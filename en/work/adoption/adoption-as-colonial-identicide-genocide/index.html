Adoption as colonial identicide genocide

Adoption as colonial identicide genocide analyses modern adoption as a form of structural violence that delocalises, alienates and indoctrinates in order to eliminate origins, roots and genealogies.

The text defines modern adoption as a technology of ontological erasure: a device of power that destroys filiation of origin, substitutes natal identity and turns kinship into material administered by the State.

Beginning with the Minnesota Adoption Act of 1917, the article places adoption within a colonial, biopolitical and eugenic genealogy. Against the adoptive myth, it proposes a realist reading: recovering name, origin and memory as acts of decolonisation and resistance.

What this text proposes

This article argues that modern adoption cannot be understood as a neutral institution of child protection, but as a historical device of identity substitution.

Its function is not limited to moving a child from one family to another. Its core is deeper: to erase a filiation, interrupt a genealogy, substitute an origin and produce a new identity under State authority.

From this perspective, adoption appears not as a humanitarian exception but as a civilised continuation of colonial forms of dispossession. The body is not necessarily eliminated, but origin, memory and belonging are.

The text proposes calling this structure colonial identicide genocide.

Central thesis

The central thesis is that modern adoption constitutes structural violence that eliminates origins, destroys genealogies and substitutes identities under State authority.

The text argues that adoption transforms filiation into an administrative construction and kinship into a matter of government. What is presented as protection operates, in reality, as a technology of ontological erasure.

The identicide process acts through three movements: delocalise, alienate and indoctrinate. First it separates the subject from their biographical territory; then it substitutes their consciousness with an alien narrative; finally it demands emotional adherence to the system that destroyed their roots.

Against this machinery, the recovery of name and origin is proposed as an act of decolonisation, memory and realist resistance.

Methodological note: knowing from within

The text begins from a decisive methodological premise: adoption can only be understood from within. Any external view risks reproducing the fiction that adoption imposes.

Adoption affects identity itself. For that reason, it cannot be observed as if it were a neutral object. Lived experience is not a sentimental addition, but the place where the real operation of the device becomes visible.

The adopted person is not merely an object of study. They are a living archive of the power that passed through them. Their biography makes it possible to measure the effects of the system with a precision that statistics alone cannot achieve.

The subjectivity of the conscious adopted person is not a bias to be removed. It is the most precise point of observation for analysing an institution that acts by destroying, administering and substituting human identities.

Minnesota 1917 and the origin of modern adoption

The article places the origin of the modern adoptive model in the Minnesota Adoption Act of 1917. That model introduced the logic of closed adoption: secrecy of origins, sealed records and the creation of a substitute legal filiation.

Through that operation, the State inaugurated a new form of power over filiation. The child ceased to be registered in continuity with their biological history and was reborn as a legal creation.

Modern adoption was therefore not simply a humanitarian evolution. It was a work of social engineering that turned identity into administrable material.

From the United States, the model later expanded into Europe and, after the Second World War, into international adoption. The national device of erasure became a global system of transfer, substitution and reorganisation of filiations.

Adoption as a colonial device of identicide power

Adoption, in its modern formulation, cannot be understood only as a legal or family act. It constitutes a technology of power operating at the intimate scale of filiation.

Its central violence is the abduction of identity and the imposition of a new genealogy under State authority. This operation turns individual biography into a field of biopolitical experimentation.

The adopted person does not consent to their adoption, the erasure of their name or the substitution of their genealogy. This lack of consent turns adoption into a total social experiment.

If a human being can be uprooted, rewritten and domesticated from childhood, adoption reveals itself as a test of the power to manufacture functional subjectivities.

But if the adopted person remembers, searches, resists or breaks the adoptive narrative, then the device partially fails: it shows that a core of truth remains that could not be suppressed.

Biopolitics, coloniality and necropolitics

The text articulates three critical frameworks: biopolitics, coloniality and necropolitics.

From the perspective of biopolitics, adoption appears as a technique for administering life. The State decides who may be a child, of whom, under what conditions and with what authorised memory.

From the coloniality of power and being, adoption is understood as the production of subjectivities. It does not merely transfer bodies: it substitutes worlds, languages, lineages, memories and forms of belonging.

From the perspective of necropolitics, adoption can be read as a soft form of civil death: it does not necessarily destroy the body, but deprives the subject of history, genealogy and symbolic continuity.

Taken together, these frameworks make it possible to understand adoption as a colonial technology of erasure: a modern form of governing life through the administration of origin.

The theory of identicide genocide

The theory of identicide genocide argues that modern adoption constitutes a complex form of colonial violence oriented towards the collective elimination of groups of origin and the identity substitution of their individuals.

Identicide names the subjective dimension of that violence: the civil and ontological death of the biographical self, replaced by an identity fabricated by the State.

Genocide, in this framework, names the collective dimension: the destruction of lineages, communities, memories and maternal continuities. The removal of children affects not only each separated child; it also affects mothers, families and peoples of origin.

Modern adoption thus operates on three levels: collectively, by emptying communities of descendants; individually, by erasing the adopted person’s identity; and epistemically, by imposing a legal truth that suppresses actual history.

This triple operation configures a total violence: it delocalises, alienates and indoctrinates.

Delocalise, alienate, indoctrinate

The identicide process is articulated through three successive and complementary operations.

Delocalise means separating the subject from their biographical, family, cultural and genealogical territory. Delocalisation produces a vacuum of origin that can be occupied by an alien identity.

Alienate means substituting the self. The adopted person learns to narrate themselves through an administrative filiation and to recognise themselves in an image produced by the system that dispossessed them.

Indoctrinate means internalising the imposed filiation. The subject not only accepts their adoptive identity, but may come to defend it, reproduce it and transmit it as the only possible truth.

These three phases reveal the real function of the adoptive device: not to rescue lives, but to reconfigure the bonds that sustain them, transforming loss into virtue and substitution into salvation.

Psychosocial and political consequences

The text analyses the psychic, social and political consequences of adoptive identicide. Trauma appears not as an accidental effect, but as a functional product of a system that requires the adopted person to live within an imposed identity.

The search for origin is interpreted as an attempt to reintegrate denied memory and restore the coherence of the self. But that search is often denied, pathologised or treated with suspicion.

The legal and health systems participate in that revictimisation when they protect closed archives, validate false filiations or interpret the adopted person’s suffering as individual pathology.

Adoption is thus immunised by the myth of goodness. The adopted person who speaks is not recognised as a victim, but treated as ungrateful, deviant or problematic.

This public denial constitutes an added violence: it turns trauma into silence and the truth of origin into a threat to social consensus.

Realism as resistance

The article contrasts realism with adoptive magical thinking. Adoption is sustained by a collective illusion that turns violence into love, abduction into rescue and loss into destiny.

Magical thinking allows institutions, adoptive families and society to act without guilt, protected by a moral narrative that transforms appropriation into virtue.

Against that spell, realism appears as an act of rupture. It does not mean coldness or cynicism, but fidelity to lived truth.

Realism means looking without veils at what the system orders us not to see: harm, substitution, dispossession, alienation and the lie turned into kinship.

For the adopted person, realism is a form of decolonisation of the gaze. It means ceasing to perform the role of the rescued child and assuming the position of a lucid survivor.

Memory, name and origin

The adopted person’s memory is not a private narrative without political value. It is a legitimate source of knowledge.

Every adopted person who searches for their origin challenges a State architecture of forgetting. Every recovered name, reconstructed filiation and corrected document breaks part of the identicide device.

Recovering origin is not nostalgia. It is restitution. It does not seek to return to the past, but to restore continuity between life and history.

In that sense, memory becomes resistance: memory against charity, truth against myth, origin against substitution.

Where power sought to write silence, the adopted person’s word appears as proof of existence.

Structure of the text

The document is organised as an extended article of historical, philosophical, political and testimonial analysis.

It begins with a methodological note defending the situated knowledge of the adopted person as a legitimate form of analysis. It then presents the historical context of modern adoption and its origin in the closed-adoption model of Minnesota in 1917.

It goes on to develop adoption as a colonial device of identicide power, articulates its theoretical framework through biopolitics, coloniality and necropolitics, and formulates the theory of identicide genocide.

The later chapters analyse the genealogy of the modern adoptive model, the identicide process —delocalise, alienate, indoctrinate—, its psychosocial and political consequences, and realism as a form of resistance.

The text concludes with a defence of lucidity, memory and truth as forms of decolonising adopted consciousness.
